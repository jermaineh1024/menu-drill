New here? How the place works
  • 13

    Every sandwich has a number, and guests order by number. "A regular 13, Mike's Way" means a regular size, sub number 13, with the standard toppings.

  • A sub is bread, cheese, meat, then toppings. The number tells you which cheese and meat. The toppings are the same seven things on almost every cold sub, and that set is called Mike's Way: onions, lettuce, tomatoes, red wine vinegar, olive oil blend, oregano, salt. In that order.

  • ColdHot

    Cold subs are deli meat sliced fresh in front of the guest. Hot subs are steak or chicken cooked on a flat grill, cheesesteak style.

  • The counter runs like a line with three jobs. The slicer slices the cheese and meat onto the bread. The sprinkler puts Mike's Way and any extras on. The wrapper closes it, cuts it, wraps it and rings it up.

  • Your job is to hear a number and know what goes on it, in what order. That is all this page trains. Prices, the register and the grill timing come from your trainer.

How the numbers work

  • 14 and under is cold. 16 and up is hot.
  • Your board's cold subs: 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14. Hot subs: 16, 17, 26, 42, 43, 44, 55, 56, plus Mike's Hot Italian for a limited time. The gaps are numbers your board doesn't carry. The chain never renumbers, so a number that leaves the board just leaves a gap.
  • Hot subs that come both ways are pairs, and chicken always takes the lower number: 16/17, 42/43, 55/56.
  • Provolone is the default cheese on cold subs. White American is the default on hot subs. Swiss shows up twice: #9 and #14.
  • Mayo is not part of Mike's Way. The two clubs, #8 and #9, get mayo and bacon automatically, on the lid.

Sizes, breads, lingo

Mike's Way
Onions, lettuce, tomatoes, red wine vinegar, olive oil blend, oregano, salt. In that order. Shorthand: O-L-T, juice, shake.
The Juice
The vinegar and oil. Some stores keep it premixed in one squeeze bottle. "Juice on the side" is a real request.
Soufflé cup
The small portion cup. Lettuce is measured in them: 2 on a mini, 3 on a regular, 5 on a giant.
Sizes
Mini (about 5 inches), Regular (about 7 to 8), Giant (about 14 to 15, two regulars, cut in four).
Breads
White, wheat, rosemary parm, and Udi's gluten free (costs extra). Any regular sub also comes as a wrap, or in a bowl: no bread, same stuff in a bowl. Some people call that a tub.
Kids meal
Kids-size turkey and cheese or ham and cheese, with a drink and a cookie.
Cheeses
Provolone, Swiss, white American.
Extras
Only if the guest asks. Never offer them. Pickles, banana peppers, jalapeños, mayo, mustard, cherry pepper relish, bacon.
Positions
Slicer, sprinkler, wrapper. Two people topping one sub together is the sprinkle dance.
"Tables"
Someone, usually the slicer, wants the dining room checked. More on the Orientation tab.
Cappacuolo
Say cap-uh-COLE-oh. Spicy cured pork shoulder. Jersey people call it gabagool.
Prosciuttini
Say pro-shoo-TEE-nee. Ham with a black pepper crust.
The slicer
The station and the person. Eighteen and over only on the meat slicer. Meat is sliced onto the scale, and the weight per size comes from the store's chart, so learn the chart from your trainer rather than counting slices.
LTO
Limited time offer. The specials on the board that rotate in and out.
Shore Points
The rewards app. Points come from the size of the sub, not the price.

Notes from the line

What the trainer called out on the floor. These matter more than anything in a video.

  1. Ask "Mike's Way?" before any toppings go on. The answer decides everything the sprinkler does next, so get it first.
  2. Mike's Way first, then the extras. Onions, lettuce, tomatoes, juice, shake. Only after that: pickles and peppers on the tomatoes, mayo and sauces on the lid.

How the counter flows

Cold sub, start to finish

The slicer puts the cheese and the meat on the bread. The sprinkler puts everything else on. The navy numbers are the seven Mike's Way steps.

  1. Take the order: size, number, bread. Then ask "Mike's Way?"
  2. Bread comes out and gets sliced open at the slicer.
  3. Cheese goes on first, straight onto the bread. Provolone unless the sub says Swiss.
  4. Meats are sliced to order onto the scale, then onto the cheese. The store's chart gives the weight for each size.
  5. Slide it to the sprinkler.
  6. Onions
  7. Lettuce
  8. Tomatoes
  9. Red wine vinegar
  10. Olive oil blend
  11. Oregano
  12. Salt
  13. Extras, only if the guest asked. Never offer them. Pickles, banana peppers and jalapeños go on top of the tomatoes. Mayo, mustard, cherry pepper relish and bacon go on the lid. The clubs, #8 and #9, get mayo and bacon on the lid automatically.
  14. The wrapper closes it, cuts it, wraps it. Giants get cut in four.

Put Mike's Way in order

The slicer has done the cheese and meat. Tap the seven in the order they go on, and watch them stack.

Hot sub, start to finish

  1. Take the order: size, number, bread. Hot subs don't get Mike's Way unless the guest insists, and most regulars know not to.
  2. Grill the steak or chicken with whatever that number grills: onions and peppers for the pairs, mushrooms and jalapeños added on the Big Kahunas.
  3. Cheese on top of the meat while it's on the grill, so it melts. White American. The Big Kahunas get extra.
  4. Into the bread in one flip.
  5. Toppings and sauce after: lettuce, tomato and the sauce on #26 and #44; chipotle mayo on #42 and #43; grilled onions, lettuce, tomato and chopped pepper relish on Mike's Hot Italian.
  6. Cut it, wrap it.

The grill has its own timing that your trainer shows you in person. What this page gets you is knowing what belongs on each number before you ever stand at the flat top.

What guests say and what it means

  • "Mike's Way" means all seven. If someone lists onions, lettuce, tomato, oil and vinegar one at a time, that's Mike's Way; build it the same.
  • "Mike's Way, no onions" is how people subtract. Build everything else as normal.
  • "Everything" at this counter means Mike's Way, not mayo and pickles. Ask if you're not sure.
  • "The Italian" is the #13. "The Philly" is the #17. "A club" needs a follow-up: #8 or #9?
  • "In a bowl" or "in a tub" means no bread. "Make it a wrap" means the same fillings in a wrap.
  • Never ask if they want extras. Listen, and they'll tell you.
  • "Mini, regular, giant." Ask size first, because the slicer weighs by size.

Food safety

The two questions the food safety video opens with: if you're sick, will you stay home? Will you wash your hands?

Washing your hands

  1. Warm running water.
  2. Soap about the size of a quarter.
  3. Scrub hands, wrists and arms at least 20 seconds. Singing the ABCs times it. Get under the nails.
  4. Rinse all the soap off, dry, and turn the faucet off with the towel.

Wash after: the restroom, sneezing, taking out trash, cleaning tables, touching your clothes, hair, nose, skin or mouth, handling raw meat, raw chicken or raw vegetables, any cleaning, and handling money. If you think you need to, you do.

Gloves

  • Wash your hands before you put them on. Pick ones that fit.
  • Change them when they're dirty or torn, when you switch tasks, after raw meat (roast beef, Philly steak, chicken), and when you get interrupted.
  • Never touch money with gloves on and then touch food. Wash and put on new gloves. Guests watch for this.

Stay home if you have

Fever, vomiting, diarrhea, sore throat with fever, or jaundice (yellow skin).

Report to the health department if you're diagnosed with hepatitis A, E. coli, salmonella, shigella or norovirus. Norovirus is the leading cause of foodborne illness in the U.S.

A cut or sore: bandage it and cover it with a glove or clothing, and you can work.

The enemy and its five weapons

The enemy is microorganisms: bacteria, viruses, molds and mildews.

  1. Time and temperature abuse. Food sitting between 41°F and 135°F too long, or not cooked to temperature, like the roast beef.
  2. Cross-contamination. Germs moving from one surface or food to another.
  3. Poor personal hygiene. Dirty hands, dirty gloves or apron, bare hands on food, working sick.
  4. Poor cleaning and sanitation. Dirty surfaces spread germs and draw pests.
  5. Not washing produce. Onions, lettuce, celery and tomatoes can carry shigella, and produce isn't cooked, so washing is the only fix.

The numbers from the video: over 48 million cases of foodborne illness and 3,000 deaths in the U.S. every year.

Uniform
  • Logo hat or visor, hair pulled back, nothing over your face, forehead or ears.
  • One stud earring per ear. Other visible piercings out. Gauges must be flesh tone.
  • Clean shaven every shift. A beard, goatee or mustache is fine if it's trimmed to a quarter inch or less and you shave around it every shift.
  • Short, clean nails. No fake nails.
  • Uniform shirt buttoned except the top button, clean, no wrinkles. Undershirt white or navy. Long sleeves under it must be form fitting.
  • Clean navy apron. Long navy khakis with a brown or black belt. No shorts.
  • Closed shoes: sneakers or brown or black non-slip, with socks. No sandals, clogs or Crocs.
  • One plain band ring. No watches, smartwatches or bracelets. Necklaces tucked under the shirt.
  • Phone out of the guests' sight. No earbuds or headphones.

Business basics: the P&L

A profit and loss statement shows, every month, whether the store made or lost money. Picture one dollar of sales getting torn up:

Top line
Sales, 100%.
Cost of goods used
Food, beverage and paper, 30%. The usual industry name is cost of goods sold, COGS.
Labor
20%: hourly crew 12 to 14%, salaried managers 6 to 8%.
Variable costs
Goods plus labor, 50%. They move with sales, and how well the store runs controls them.
Fixed costs
Expenses like rent and utilities, 30%. Same every month no matter the sales.
Bottom line
Profit or loss. The goal is 20%.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization. The 20% still has those to pay, so the store doesn't keep all of it.

Checking tables

  • It's a towel, never a rag. Keep them in the red sanitation bucket near the dining room.
  • Two towels: one for tabletops, one for seats. Keep them out of guests' sight.
  • Never take a spray bottle out to the tables.
  • Wipe crumbs into your hand, wipe the seats, reset the chairs.
  • Stock and wipe the fountain counter. Empty the trash with a helper so it's quick.
  • Spot sweep, but never by a guest's feet. Come back after they leave.
  • Both bathrooms stocked, working and sparkling.
  • Greet anyone walking by, and get the door when you can.
  • Dining room first, kitchen second.

FIFO: first in, first out

Oldest product gets used first. That's the whole idea, and it runs through every storage area in the store.

  • Deliveries (Sysco, produce, Pepsi): label everything with the receipt date, then rotate oldest first.
  • Meat case: weigh for inventory and date it when it comes in. Swap the display out by FIFO, and whatever leaves the case goes back in the walk-in cooler in FIFO order too.
  • Prepped food not used within 24 hours gets labeled with its expiration date. Tuna is the classic example.
  • Sauce bottles: never refill. Use it till it's empty, wash, rinse, sanitize, then fill. Never new sauce on top of old. Keep a spare set of clean bottles.
  • Vinegar and oil bottles: empty, wash, rinse and sanitize at least once a week.
  • Restocking the drop-in (the chilled well on the line that holds the topping pans) or the sandwich unit: fresh pan, new product on the bottom, existing product on top. Fill backups about 3/4 full. Restock on the back counter or in the kitchen, never straight into the drop-in.
  • Chip racks, soda case and cookies rotate the same way.

Thermometers

Temps get taken 3 times a day and logged on the daily checklist. Logs are kept 3 months, which is what protects the store if anyone ever claims they got sick.

Bimetallic stem
The cheap probe with the dial. Needs constant calibration by the ice-point method: in a cup of ice water it should read 32°F. It senses about 1 inch up from the tip, at the dimple, so the dimple has to be in the food.
Digital probe
Your best bet for accuracy, needs very little calibrating. Senses along the last inch of the stem; that whole inch goes in the food.
Digital oven probe
For cooking roast beef and heating meatballs. Stays in the oven or pan, reads out on a screen, has a timer. Delicate; keep a backup, and try new batteries before buying a new one.
Infrared gun
Surface temps only, like the grill top. Never for an internal temperature.
  • Wash, rinse and sanitize the thermometer before use and between uses. Run three at once so you're not walking back and forth to the sink.
  • Aim for the center or thickest part, and get the whole sensing area in.
  • Air temp and the equipment thermostat are not product temp. Only temp the actual food.
  • The log lives on the behind-the-counter site (the staff website), under Food Safety.

Cleaning and sanitizing

Cleaning removes food and dirt. Sanitizing brings germs down to safe levels. Two different things, always in that order.

  • A food contact surface is anything that touches food: prep tables, the prep sink, utensils, knives, pans, containers, cutting boards, the slicer.
  • Clean and sanitize before and after use, and every 4 hours of continuous use. The slicer gets fully taken apart for it: after prep, after lunch rush, before dinner rush, and at close.
  • Any vomiting or diarrhea in the store is treated as a possible norovirus event. There's a biohazard spill kit and a playbook (Food Safety Mike's Way 2.0) for it.

The three-compartment sink

Chlorine
50 to 200 parts per million.
Quats
Quaternary ammonium, 200 to 500 parts per million.
Test strips
Test every time if you free-pour. Too little doesn't kill enough; too much is a chemical hazard. The health inspector looks for the strips.
Change the water
When it's dirty or the sanitizer tests weak.
Knives
Take a knife all the way through wash, rinse, sanitize. Never leave one in the sink.
Red buckets
Same sanitizer as the sink, in a clean, sanitized red bucket, filled 1/3, towels submerged. One for the slicer, one shared by sprinkler and wrapper, one for the grill, one for prep, one for the dining room. It loses strength fast, so test and swap often.

Pests and contaminants
  • Pests need food and shelter. Clean thoroughly, keep outside doors closed, keep a pest company under contract, and report any sign to the manager right away. Never apply pesticide yourself.
  • Biological hazards: bacteria, viruses, molds, mildews. Chemical: detergents, too-strong sanitizer, pesticides, cleaners, allergens. Physical: anything foreign in the food: jewelry, a nail, a band-aid, a staple, glass, metal, and the most common one, hair. Hat or visor on, hair pulled back.
  • If you spot any of those in product, throw the product out.

Washing produce

Produce isn't cooked, so washing is the only thing that removes shigella. Prep and rinse onions, lettuce and tomatoes the day before they're sliced.

Onions
Peel, then rinse well, especially the cut ends. Don't cut deep when prepping; a deep cut is a place for germs to hide.
Lettuce
Pull off and toss the outer leaves. Rinse the head under cold water core side down, around it, never pouring water into it.
Tomatoes
Wash each one under cool water. Never slice a tomato straight out of the box. Only sliced if it's been washed.
Celery
Cut off the top of the bunch, cut the bunch in half at the core, de-leaf. Rinse downward between the stalks, 30 seconds per half, 1 minute total. Slice with the guard, no rushing, to 3/16 inch (tomato thickness), and don't slice the last 2 inches.
Also rinse
Lemons, green peppers, and avocados where the store carries them.

Cutting the sub

Knife in your dominant hand. Clean it when needed, and always after tuna or a sub with mayo. If you cut the paper, finish wrapping and wrap it again with a second sheet.

  1. 1

    Cup, up, down. Cup your hand with all five fingers over the blade, bring the knife up to your hand, then down on the sub. Gently press down on the sub's lid and cut with a sawing motion, quickly and neatly, without cutting the paper. Watch your thumb and pinky: they get hurt the most.

  2. 2

    How many pieces. Minis in half, regulars in half, giants in quarters. Don't ask how they want it cut. If a guest asks for six, cut it in half and each half in thirds. For eight, cut quarters and then each quarter in half. Take the moment to make the pieces equal.

Wrapping the sub

You're looking straight down at the counter: the guest is at the top of each picture, you are at the bottom. Red is the motion. Plain white is the side of the paper facing up; the patterned side is the logo side, which ends up on the outside. Drawn from the video's words, so if your trainer does a step differently, your trainer wins.

  1. Start

    Tear one sheet. Grab the paper from the edge of the roll with your dominant hand, not the middle, with your other hand on the counter. Pull it to the length for the sub and tear. Mini or kids meal 12 inches, regular 15, giant 21. One sheet at a time, logo side down.

  2. 1

    Four fingers underneath, thumbs on top. Slide your fingers under each end of the sub and put your thumbs on top. Always grab it by the ends. Keep it close to the counter so it holds together, and set it in the very center of the paper on the diagonal, the ends of the sub matched to the corners.

  3. 2

    Cut it, then check the center. Cut the sub the way Cutting the sub shows: hand cupped over the blade, sawing gently, without cutting the paper. After cutting, make sure the sub is still centered on the paper.

  4. 3

    Pinch, pull over, punch. Pinch the corner of the paper closest to you, thumb underneath and fingers on top. Bring it over the sub and hold it against the paper and the counter with the palm of your hand. On a giant, check the outside corners, since they sometimes move.

  5. 4

    Grab, pull back. Put your other hand on top of the sub and pull back on the sub to secure the paper. Pulling back releases the paper from your first hand, and the paper now completely covers the sub.

  6. 5

    Quarter turn. Swing your thumbs behind and under the sub. With a secure grip and your thumbs perpendicular to the sub, use both hands to roll it forward a quarter turn. It is now on its side with your fingers underneath.

  7. 6

    Fold it in, fold it in. Place your thumb on the end of the sub, roll your fingers over your thumb, then hold the fold in place. That tucks the paper and leaves a rounded end. Repeat on the other side.

  8. 7

    Roll forward: bottom, side, top. Both hands on the sub, fingers spread wide and thumbs perpendicular to the sub, which is the key to a tight roll. Roll it forward quickly: that's the bottom of the sub, roll again for the side, roll again for the top. Tight, without crushing the sub.

  9. 8

    Finish in your dominant hand. Keep rolling and bring the sub into your dominant hand, anchoring the flap with your index finger. The sub is wrapped neatly with the ends tucked in, and the top of the sub is in your hand. Always finish in your dominant hand.

Bagging the sub

Popping and creasing the bag are part of the show. The pictures look down at the counter with the guest at the top.

  1. 1

    Pop the bag forward. Press down with your fingers on the stack of bags a few inches from the open end, which opens the top bag just enough to slide your thumb in. Press your thumb against your pointer and middle fingers and lift the bag. Extend your arm and flick your wrist so the bag pops open. Always pop it forward, where guests can see and hear it, never down or back.

  2. 2

    Twist it into the bag. With the wrapped sub in your dominant hand, give it a little twist as it first goes into the bag, keeping everything over the counter. The top of the sub goes on the top of the bag and the bottom of the sub on the bottom of the bag.

  3. 3

    Crease it, roll it over. Place the bag logo side down on the counter. Pinch the bag along the top and press it into the counter to make a crease. Then roll the sub over the crease toward the guest until the logo side is up. The crease seals the bag around the sub.

  4. 4

    Twist it, mark it. For regulars, minis and kids meals, add a twist after the sub is rolled over. Keep a marker handy: mark the number of the sub on the bag when there are multiple subs on an order, and always mark the name for a phone, online or catering order. Bags can be marked ahead while the subs are being sliced and sprinkled.

The register and the wrapper
  • Peripheral hearing. Listen in when the guest orders at the slicer, then tell the cashier: "Number five, come down."
  • Direct traffic. Someone at the register who hasn't ordered gets walked to the slicer. Someone looking lost may have ordered ahead: "Did you place an online order?"
  • Never pull a guest down to the register before their cold sub is made. They just get pulled back, and it confuses everyone.
  • The wrapper helps the whole line go faster: restock, jump in on sprinkling, stay heads up.
  • Celebrate the sub, and say a real thank you.
