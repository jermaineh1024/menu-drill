New here? How the place works
  • 13

    Every sandwich has a number, and guests order by number. "A regular 13, Mike's Way" means a regular size, sub number 13, with the standard toppings.

  • A sub is bread, cheese, meat, then toppings. The number tells you which cheese and meat. The toppings are the same seven things on almost every cold sub, and that set is called Mike's Way: onions, lettuce, tomatoes, red wine vinegar, olive oil blend, oregano, salt. In that order.

  • ColdHot

    Cold subs are deli meat sliced fresh in front of the guest. Hot subs are steak or chicken cooked on a flat grill, cheesesteak style.

  • The counter runs like a line with three jobs. The slicer slices the cheese and meat onto the bread. The sprinkler puts Mike's Way and any extras on. The wrapper closes it, cuts it, wraps it and rings it up.

  • Your job is to hear a number and know what goes on it, in what order. That comes first, and it's what Study and Drill train. The Training tab then goes station by station: register, prep, bread, the grill, the slicer.

How the numbers work

  • 14 and under is cold. 16 and up is hot.
  • Your board's cold subs: 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14. Hot subs: 16, 17, 26, 42, 43, 44, 55, 56, plus Mike's Hot Italian for a limited time. The gaps are numbers your board doesn't carry. The chain never renumbers, so a number that leaves the board just leaves a gap.
  • Hot subs that come both ways are pairs, and chicken always takes the lower number: 16/17, 42/43, 55/56.
  • Provolone is the default cheese on cold subs. White American is the default on hot subs. Swiss shows up twice: #9 and #14.
  • Mayo is not part of Mike's Way. The two clubs, #8 and #9, get mayo and bacon automatically, on the lid.

How the counter flows

Cold sub, start to finish

The slicer puts the cheese and the meat on the bread. The sprinkler puts everything else on. The navy numbers are the seven Mike's Way steps.

  1. Take the order: size and number. Don't ask about bread: it's white unless the guest says otherwise. Then ask "Mike's Way?"
  2. Bread gets cut open at the slicer, into equal halves, starting at the round end closest to the guest. A few loaves are pre-cut before the rush.
  3. Cheese goes on first, straight onto the bread. Provolone unless the sub says Swiss.
  4. Meats are sliced to order straight onto the sub, in the order the slice guide lists them, covering it end to end.
  5. Slide it to the sprinkler.
  6. Onions
  7. Lettuce
  8. Tomatoes
  9. Red wine vinegar
  10. Olive oil blend
  11. Oregano
  12. Salt
  13. Extras, only if the guest asked. Never offer them. Pickles, banana peppers and jalapeños go on top of the tomatoes. Mayo, mustard, cherry pepper relish and bacon go on the lid. The clubs, #8 and #9, get mayo and bacon on the lid automatically.
  14. The wrapper closes it, cuts it, wraps it. Giants get cut in four.

Hot sub, start to finish

The order the grill video builds them in. The lid gets dressed before the meat ever hits the grill, and lettuce goes on last.

  1. Take the order: size and number. Don't ask about peppers and onions; a guest who wants it changed will say so. Hot subs don't get Mike's Way unless the guest asks for it.
  2. Bread on the grill. It's pre-cut into a pocket. Bottom side down, turned often: warm, not toasted.
  3. Paper and cheese ready. 15 inches of paper for a regular, 21 for a giant. Lay out 3 slices of white American, 4 for a Big Kahuna.
  4. The lid first, if the sub has one. 3 lines of sauce on the inside of the lid: chipotle mayo on #42 and #43, ranch on #26, blue cheese dressing on #44. Then bacon on the #26, then tomatoes on #26 and #44. Set the bread aside.
  5. On the grill. On the pairs: onions, peppers on top of them, then the steak or chicken. Mushrooms and jalapeños go down too on the Big Kahunas. #26 and #44 are chicken alone.
  6. Break it: 4 times, bring it together, 4 more, onions and peppers on top, 8 more.
  7. Sauce on the meat, on one sub: the #44 gets 3 full rows of Frank's RedHot on the cooked chicken.
  8. Cheese on top to melt, with a few slits cut in it.
  9. Onto the bread, pushed onto the spatula with the dough cutter. Scrape the loose bits on.
  10. Lettuce to finish on #26 and #44: 2 soufflé cups on a regular, 4 on a giant.
  11. Wrap it loosely in the hot sub wrap, standing on its side. Giants are cut in half. Regulars aren't cut.

Every step is on the Training tab, under The grill, with pictures and amounts. Mike's Hot Italian isn't in the grill video, so ask your trainer where its toppings go.

What guests say and what it means

  • "Mike's Way" means all seven. If someone lists onions, lettuce, tomato, oil and vinegar one at a time, that's Mike's Way; build it the same.
  • "Mike's Way, no onions" is how people subtract. Build everything else as normal.
  • "Everything" at this counter means Mike's Way, not mayo and pickles. Ask if you're not sure.
  • "The Italian" is the #13. "The Philly" is the #17. "A club" needs a follow-up: #8 or #9?
  • "In a bowl" or "in a tub" means no bread. "Make it a wrap" means the same fillings in a wrap.
  • Never ask if they want extras. Listen, and they'll tell you.
  • "Mini, regular, giant." Get the size first: the portion and the paper both depend on it.
  • No bread named means white. No changes named on a hot sub means it comes the way the board lists it.

Food safety

The two questions the food safety video opens with: if you're sick, will you stay home? Will you wash your hands?

Washing your hands

  1. Warm running water.
  2. Soap about the size of a quarter.
  3. Scrub hands, wrists and arms at least 20 seconds. Singing the ABCs times it. Get under the nails.
  4. Rinse all the soap off, dry, and turn the faucet off with the towel.

Wash after: the restroom, sneezing, taking out trash, cleaning tables, touching your clothes, hair, nose, skin or mouth, handling raw meat, raw chicken or raw vegetables, any cleaning, and handling money. If you think you need to, you do.

Gloves

  • Wash your hands before you put them on. Pick ones that fit.
  • Change them when they're dirty or torn, when you switch tasks, after raw meat (roast beef, Philly steak, chicken), and when you get interrupted.
  • Never touch money with gloves on and then touch food. Wash and put on new gloves. Guests watch for this.

Stay home if you have

Fever, vomiting, diarrhea, sore throat with fever, or jaundice (yellow skin).

Report to the health department if you're diagnosed with hepatitis A, E. coli, salmonella, shigella or norovirus. Norovirus is the leading cause of foodborne illness in the U.S.

A cut or sore: bandage it and cover it with a glove or clothing, and you can work.

The enemy and its five weapons

The enemy is microorganisms: bacteria, viruses, molds and mildews.

  1. Time and temperature abuse. Food sitting between 41°F and 135°F too long, or not cooked to temperature, like the roast beef.
  2. Cross-contamination. Germs moving from one surface or food to another.
  3. Poor personal hygiene. Dirty hands, dirty gloves or apron, bare hands on food, working sick.
  4. Poor cleaning and sanitation. Dirty surfaces spread germs and draw pests.
  5. Not washing produce. Onions, lettuce, celery and tomatoes can carry shigella, and produce isn't cooked, so washing is the only fix.

The numbers from the video: over 48 million cases of foodborne illness and 3,000 deaths in the U.S. every year.

Uniform
  • Logo hat or visor, hair pulled back, nothing over your face, forehead or ears.
  • One stud earring per ear. Other visible piercings out. Gauges must be flesh tone.
  • Clean shaven every shift. A beard, goatee or mustache is fine if it's trimmed to a quarter inch or less and you shave around it every shift.
  • Short, clean nails. No fake nails.
  • Uniform shirt buttoned except the top button, clean, no wrinkles. Undershirt white or navy. Long sleeves under it must be form fitting.
  • Clean navy apron. Long navy khakis with a brown or black belt. No shorts.
  • Closed shoes: sneakers or brown or black non-slip, with socks. No sandals, clogs or Crocs.
  • One plain band ring. No watches, smartwatches or bracelets. Necklaces tucked under the shirt.
  • Phone out of the guests' sight. No earbuds or headphones.

Business basics: the P&L

A profit and loss statement shows, every month, whether the store made or lost money. Picture one dollar of sales getting torn up:

Top line
Sales, 100%.
Cost of goods used
Food, beverage and paper, 30%. The usual industry name is cost of goods sold, COGS.
Labor
20%: hourly crew 12 to 14%, salaried managers 6 to 8%.
Variable costs
Goods plus labor, 50%. They move with sales, and how well the store runs controls them.
Fixed costs
Expenses like rent and utilities, 30%. Same every month no matter the sales.
Bottom line
Profit or loss. The goal is 20%.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization. The 20% still has those to pay, so the store doesn't keep all of it.

Kitchen and food safety What keeps the food safe behind the line.

FIFO: first in, first out

Oldest product gets used first. That's the whole idea, and it runs through every storage area in the store.

  • Deliveries (Sysco, produce, Pepsi): label everything with the receipt date, then rotate oldest first.
  • Meat case: weigh for inventory and date it when it comes in. Swap the display out by FIFO, and whatever leaves the case goes back in the walk-in cooler in FIFO order too.
  • Prepped food not used within 24 hours gets labeled with its expiration date. Tuna is the classic example.
  • Sauce bottles: never refill. Use it till it's empty, wash, rinse, sanitize, then fill. Never new sauce on top of old. Keep a spare set of clean bottles.
  • Vinegar and oil bottles: empty, wash, rinse and sanitize at least once a week.
  • Restocking the drop-in (the chilled well on the line that holds the topping pans) or the sandwich unit: fresh pan, new product on the bottom, existing product on top. Fill backups about 3/4 full. Restock on the back counter or in the kitchen, never straight into the drop-in.
  • Chip racks, soda case and cookies rotate the same way.

Thermometers

Temps get taken 3 times a day and logged on the daily checklist. Logs are kept 3 months, which is what protects the store if anyone ever claims they got sick.

Bimetallic stem
The cheap probe with the dial. Needs constant calibration by the ice-point method: in a cup of ice water it should read 32°F. It senses about 1 inch up from the tip, at the dimple, so the dimple has to be in the food.
Digital probe
Your best bet for accuracy, needs very little calibrating. Senses along the last inch of the stem; that whole inch goes in the food.
Digital oven probe
For cooking roast beef and heating meatballs. Stays in the oven or pan, reads out on a screen, has a timer. Delicate; keep a backup, and try new batteries before buying a new one.
Infrared gun
Surface temps only, like the grill top. Never for an internal temperature.
  • Wash, rinse and sanitize the thermometer before use and between uses. Run three at once so you're not walking back and forth to the sink.
  • Aim for the center or thickest part, and get the whole sensing area in.
  • Air temp and the equipment thermostat are not product temp. Only temp the actual food.
  • The log lives on the behind-the-counter site (the staff website), under Food Safety.

Hot, cold and cooling

Bacteria grow fast between 41°F and 135°F. The whole game is how little time food spends there: keep hot foods hot and cold foods cold.

  • 4 hours is the limit. Food that has spent 4 hours or more, in total, in the danger zone gets thrown out.
  • Heating: meatballs, tomato sauce and soups are heated, and reheated, to 165°F inside for 15 seconds.
  • Holding hot: once heated, they hold at 135°F or higher in the steam table.
  • Roast beef follows its own video and chart. The key: it holds 140°F for 12 minutes.
  • Holding cold: 41°F or below.
  • Cooling (roast beef, meatballs, tomato sauce, soups): from 135°F down to 70°F or below within 2 hours, then from 70°F down to 41°F or below within the next 4. Six hours in all.

Cleaning and sanitizing

Cleaning removes food and dirt. Sanitizing brings germs down to safe levels. Two different things, always in that order.

  • A food contact surface is anything that touches food: prep tables, the prep sink, utensils, knives, pans, containers, cutting boards, the slicer.
  • Clean and sanitize before and after use, and every 4 hours of continuous use. The slicer gets fully taken apart for it: after prep, after lunch rush, before dinner rush, and at close.
  • Any vomiting or diarrhea in the store is treated as a possible norovirus event. There's a biohazard spill kit and a playbook (Food Safety Mike's Way 2.0) for it.

The three-compartment sink

Chlorine
50 to 200 parts per million.
Quats
Quaternary ammonium, 200 to 500 parts per million.
Test strips
Test every time if you free-pour. Too little doesn't kill enough; too much is a chemical hazard. The health inspector looks for the strips.
Change the water
When it's dirty or the sanitizer tests weak.
Knives
Take a knife all the way through wash, rinse, sanitize. Never leave one in the sink.
Red buckets
Same sanitizer as the sink, in a clean, sanitized red bucket, filled 1/3, towels submerged. One for the slicer, one shared by sprinkler and wrapper, one for the grill, one for prep, one for the dining room. It loses strength fast, so test and swap often.

Pests and contaminants
  • Pests need food and shelter. Clean thoroughly, keep outside doors closed, keep a pest company under contract, and report any sign to the manager right away. Never apply pesticide yourself.
  • Biological hazards: bacteria, viruses, molds, mildews. Chemical: detergents, too-strong sanitizer, pesticides, cleaners, allergens. Physical: anything foreign in the food: jewelry, a nail, a band-aid, a staple, glass, metal, and the most common one, hair. Hat or visor on, hair pulled back.
  • If you spot any of those in product, throw the product out.

Washing produce

Produce isn't cooked, so washing is the only thing that removes shigella. Prep and rinse onions, lettuce and tomatoes the day before they're sliced.

Onions
Peel, then rinse well, especially the cut ends. Don't cut deep when prepping; a deep cut is a place for germs to hide.
Lettuce
Pull off and toss the outer leaves. Rinse the head under cold water core side down, around it, never pouring water into it.
Tomatoes
Wash each one under cool water. Never slice a tomato straight out of the box. Only sliced if it's been washed.
Celery
Cut off the top of the bunch, cut the bunch in half at the core, de-leaf. Rinse downward between the stalks, 30 seconds per half, 1 minute total. Slice with the guard, no rushing, to 3/16 inch (tomato thickness), and don't slice the last 2 inches.
Also rinse
Lemons, green peppers, and avocados where the store carries them.

Wrapper and register Cut, wrap, bag, ring up, thank.

Cutting the sub

Knife in your dominant hand. Clean it when needed, and always after tuna or a sub with mayo. If you cut the paper, finish wrapping and wrap it again with a second sheet.

This is the cold sub cut. Hot subs are different: giants in half, regulars not cut at all. See Wrapping hot subs, under The grill.

  1. 1

    Cup, up, down. Cup your hand with all five fingers over the blade, bring the knife up to your hand, then down on the sub. Gently press down on the sub's lid and cut with a sawing motion, quickly and neatly, without cutting the paper. Watch your thumb and pinky: they get hurt the most.

  2. 2

    How many pieces. Minis in half, regulars in half, giants in quarters. Don't ask how they want it cut. If a guest asks for six, cut it in half and each half in thirds. For eight, cut quarters and then each quarter in half. Take the moment to make the pieces equal.

Drawn from the video's words, so if your trainer does a step differently, your trainer wins.

  1. Start

    Tear one sheet. Grab the paper from the edge of the roll with your dominant hand, not the middle, with your other hand on the counter. Pull it to the length for the sub and tear. Mini or kids meal 12 inches, regular 15, giant 21. One sheet at a time, logo side down.

  2. 1

    Four fingers underneath, thumbs on top. Slide your fingers under each end of the sub and put your thumbs on top. Always grab it by the ends. Keep it close to the counter so it holds together, and set it in the very center of the paper on the diagonal, the ends of the sub matched to the corners.

  3. 2

    Cut it, then check the center. Cut the sub the way Cutting the sub shows: hand cupped over the blade, sawing gently, without cutting the paper. After cutting, make sure the sub is still centered on the paper.

  4. 3

    Pinch, pull over, punch. Pinch the corner of the paper closest to you, thumb underneath and fingers on top. Bring it over the sub and hold it against the paper and the counter with the palm of your hand. On a giant, check the outside corners, since they sometimes move.

  5. 4

    Grab, pull back. Put your other hand on top of the sub and pull back on the sub to secure the paper. Pulling back releases the paper from your first hand, and the paper now completely covers the sub.

  6. 5

    Quarter turn. Swing your thumbs behind and under the sub. With a secure grip and your thumbs perpendicular to the sub, use both hands to roll it forward a quarter turn. It is now on its side with your fingers underneath.

  7. 6

    Fold it in, fold it in. Place your thumb on the end of the sub, roll your fingers over your thumb, then hold the fold in place. That tucks the paper and leaves a rounded end. Repeat on the other side.

  8. 7

    Roll forward: bottom, side, top. Both hands on the sub, fingers spread wide and thumbs perpendicular to the sub, which is the key to a tight roll. Roll it forward quickly: that's the bottom of the sub, roll again for the side, roll again for the top. Tight, without crushing the sub.

  9. 8

    Finish in your dominant hand. Keep rolling and bring the sub into your dominant hand, anchoring the flap with your index finger. The sub is wrapped neatly with the ends tucked in, and the top of the sub is in your hand. Always finish in your dominant hand.

Bagging the sub

Popping and creasing the bag are part of the show. The pictures look down at the counter with the guest at the top.

  1. 1

    Pop the bag forward. Press down with your fingers on the stack of bags a few inches from the open end, which opens the top bag just enough to slide your thumb in. Press your thumb against your pointer and middle fingers and lift the bag. Extend your arm and flick your wrist so the bag pops open. Always pop it forward, where guests can see and hear it, never down or back.

  3. 3

    Crease it, roll it over. Place the bag logo side down on the counter. Pinch the bag along the top and press it into the counter to make a crease. Then roll the sub over the crease toward the guest until the logo side is up. The crease seals the bag around the sub.

The register and the wrapper
  • Peripheral hearing. Listen in when the guest orders at the slicer, then tell the cashier: "Number five, come down."
  • Direct traffic. Someone at the register who hasn't ordered gets walked to the slicer. Someone looking lost may have ordered ahead: "Did you place an online order?"
  • Never pull a guest down to the register before their cold sub is made. They just get pulled back, and it confuses everyone.
  • The wrapper helps the whole line go faster: restock, jump in on sprinkling, stay heads up.

The register: keeping it safe

The video's point: paying attention to guests is also what keeps the register safe.

  • Greet every guest as they walk in. Look them in the eye and ask if they need help. Being noticed can discourage a robbery.
  • Cash: lay the guest's bills on the drawer, not in the slots. Make the change, then put the bills away. If there's a question about the change, the bills they handed you are still sitting there.
  • Coins first, then bills, and count the change back as you hand it over.
  • Keep the drawer low. 50s and 100s get checked, then put away out of sight. When bills build up, tell the manager so they can make a drop.
  • Empty the tip jar between rushes.
  • Something looks off? Tell the manager and call the police. Never try to handle it yourself.
  • Wash your hands any time you leave the register. Cash and cards carry germs.

The register: the last impression

The register may be the last contact a guest has with the team, so it decides how they remember the visit.

  • Smile, eye contact, be genuine. Positive without overdoing it. Guests can tell phony.
  • Always ask if they're a rewards member. A guest who misses their points gets upset. The video calls the program Shore Points. It's MyMike's Rewards now, and the points are still called Shore Points.

Prep Produce, tuna, meats and cheeses, and how much to make.

Prep: the ground rules and how much
  • Everything is prepped fresh, every day.
  • Food safety always: washed hands, gloves, rinsed produce, temperatures.
  • Prep happens outside the rushes. Lunch is usually 11 a.m. to 2 p.m. Dinner is usually 5 to 7 p.m.
  • Customers first, prep second. Any time a guest is in the store, stop and help the team serve them.

Prepping the right amount saves time and cuts waste. Check with the manager for how much.

Onions: peel today, slice tomorrow

Peeling, the day before

  1. Cut off the root and the stem with a sanitized knife. On the root side, go just deep enough to take out the solid core. Cutting away more is waste.
  2. Slit about the first two layers along the side, and peel the skin away. Cut out any bad spots.
  3. Don't cut deep. A deep cut gives strings, not rings, when the onion is sliced, and gives germs a place to hide.
  4. Rinse under cold water at the prep sink, most of all where you cut.
  5. Into a food storage container and into the walk-in until tomorrow. These are the onions the opener slices.
  6. Wash and sanitize the work area.

Slicing

Never go by the dial. Measure against a tomato slice every time, and check again as you go.

  • Set up: slicer clean and fully assembled, a large bin for fluffing, third pans.
  • Check the thickness: slice 2 slices of onion, stack them, and hold them against a center slice of tomato.
  • Two onions at a time, similar in size, one in front of the other, either end down, with the pusher deck all the way back. Guard on top, right hand on the guard.
  • Push only the distance of the onions. If one shifts, bring the guard all the way back and set it again.
  • Slice all the way down. After each pair, move them to the bin and fluff: break them apart into rings without breaking the rings.
  • Into third pans. Lid or plastic wrap, date it, refrigerate. FIFO.

Lettuce: bop today, slice tomorrow

Bopping, the day before

  1. Bop the head core side down on a sanitized solid surface to loosen the core. Then twist and pull the core out. Only the core.
  2. Not too hard, not too soft. Too hard and the head goes loose and breaks apart. Too soft and the whole core won't come out.
  3. Pull off unusable outer leaves, as few as you can.
  4. Rinse the outside under cold water at the prep sink, core side down. Don't rinse inside the core.
  5. Core side down in a Cambro container, lid resting on top, not sealed. A little airflow helps the lettuce dry. Walk-in overnight.
  6. Wash and sanitize the work area.

Slicing

  • Lettuce is sliced after onions, so wipe the slicer down well first. Grit mat and green cutting board in place.
  • Thickness: two thirds of a center tomato slice. Set a tomato slice on the adjustable deck by the blade and open to slightly less. Never by the dial.
  • Two heads at a time, core side down. Don't reach in with your left hand. Guard on top, right hand on the guard.
  • Push only the distance of the lettuce, and don't push down. If one head is bigger, put it in front and slice it down to the size of the other, then slice through both.
  • Break it by hand on the green board: 4 times, 2 per head, with a ripping or tearing motion. Never a knife or a dough cutter. Cutting tools are too harsh on lettuce.
  • Lid or plastic wrap on the full pan, and date it.

Tomatoes: sort, rinse, slice

Sorting

  • Ripe takes both: a deep red color, and it gives a little when you squeeze it.
  • Only one of the two (red but hard, or soft but pale): the unripe case, for another day.
  • Going very soft with borderline color: the ripe case. Another day and it might go bad, so salvage what you can.
  • Lid on the unripe case, mark it P for pink, and date it.

Rinsing, the day before

  • Ripe tomatoes only, from the walk-in. Pull the stems.
  • Rinse each one under cold water at the vegetable sink.
  • Into a clean bin, covered, and back in the walk-in until tomorrow.
  • Never slice a tomato straight out of the box.

Slicing

  • Have ready: the tomatoes, enough half pans, and a clean, sanitized tomato slicer.
  • Stem side down on the slicer. Hands away from the blades.
  • One hand at the far end, where the tomato comes out, to stop it. The other on the pusher handle: push or tap it through evenly.
  • If it sticks, take it out. Forcing it bends and dulls the blades.
  • One tomato gives 8 to 9 usable slices, about a loaf and a half.

Celery and tuna

Celery

Celery carries a lot of foodborne pathogens, so the wash is the point.

  1. Chef's knife: cut the first ½ inch off the tops of all the stalks. That part is dry, discolored and dirty.
  2. Cut the bunch in half at the core, into two split bunches. Pull off all the leaves.
  3. Rinse from the core outward. Work the water between the stalks in a downward motion, so dirt washes away from the core. At least 30 seconds a split, 1 minute for the bunch.
  4. Slice at 3/16 inch, the same as a tomato. One split at a time, guard on.
  5. Stop with 2 inches left on each split. Embedded dirt gets thrown out with it, and the pieces come out more even.
  6. Tare a food prep pan on the scale and fill it to 30 oz. That's one batch of tuna.

Tip from the video: slice celery in the morning along with the onions and lettuce.

Tuna, one batch

  1. Get everything together first, so you can mix quickly.
  2. Open one pouch into a full-size Cambro bin and scrape the pouch clean.
  3. Break it down: a golf-ball-size handful between your palms, rubbed until it's fine. No chunks. One pouch at a time. The albacore is harder to break down than the light.
  4. Mix the two tunas together.
  5. Black pepper, sprinkled evenly over the top, mixed all the way in.
  6. Celery, mixed all the way in.
  7. Mayo, mixed all the way in. Every time, check the corners, the edges and the middle.
  8. Spatula it into 6-inch deep third pans with lids. Date every pan. Into the walk-in.
  9. Let it rest until the next day. It sets up and gets easier to spread.
  10. Stir it before serving. It separates as it sits.

Bread Pan it tonight, proof and bake it in the morning.

Bread 1: panning

The video's line for all three bread lessons: bread is the foundation of every sub. Here is the whole bread day, then the first step.

Panning

  • A clean bread pan and form, the form laid right on the pan.
  • 2 oz of cornmeal, sprinkled evenly in the channels of the form. Too much builds up and blocks the heat from below, so the bread won't bake right. Too little and the bread burns.
  • Frozen loaves into the channels, rib side down. Minis the same way.
  • Slide the pan carefully into the bread rack. When you're done, zip the rack cover closed. It keeps the moisture on the dough while it thaws.
  • Leftover frozen dough: close the bag in the box and get it back in the freezer right away.
  • Roll the rack into the walk-in slowly, so the dough doesn't roll in the channels.
  • Pan after 7 p.m., so the bread thaws properly overnight. Check the amount with the manager or shift leader.
  • Work fast. Frozen dough thaws quickly.

Bread 2: proofing

Proofing wakes up the yeast, so the dough rises fast.

  • Preheat both. Oven to 350°F. Proofer to 100°F at 85 to 95% relative humidity, usually a setting of 4 to 5. The hygrometer at the bottom of the proofer shows the real number.
  • A warmed-up proofer has a mist on the door, not water running down the glass.
  • Rack out of the cooler: straighten the loaves in the forms, and stretch any short ones to the length of the form.
  • First 5 trays into the top 5 rungs, timer for 25 minutes. Then 5 more trays below them, and another 25 minutes.
  • Be quick with the door. Heat and humidity get out every second it's open.
  • Check the dough now and then. It should be slightly tacky. If it looks dry, spray it with room temperature water.
  • 50 to 70 minutes in all. Proofers and the weather vary, which is where the 20 minutes comes from.
  • White and wheat are proofed separately.
  • Done when the loaf just touches the curve of the bread tool: 2 to 2.5 times its original size.

Bread 3: score, bake, cool, stack

A great bake starts with a proper proof. Nothing you do in the oven makes up for a bad one.

  • Score the white loaves, minis too, with the scoring knife: 1/16 inch deep, down the center, stopping 1.5 inches from each end. Wheat is not scored.
  • Into the oven, door closed right away. Bake 14 to 16 minutes. Wheat may take 1 to 2 minutes longer.
  • Keep the rotation going: when the top 5 rungs of the proofer empty, move the bottom 5 trays up and load 5 more below.
  • At the timer, check the color against the bread color chart on the proofer door: golden brown. Take it out the moment it gets there.
  • Cool on the bottom rungs of the bread rack. Heat rises, so the coolest bread is always on the bottom. The cover stays open while it cools; zip it only when every loaf on the rack is cool.
  • Stack only when it's completely cool. Rub the bottoms of 2 loaves together to knock off the extra cornmeal. Pyramid no more than 3 rows high, 18 to 24 loaves a pan, on the front line bread rack, covered.
  • Busy: a smaller pyramid can sit on the front counter by the slicer. Slow: keep the bread covered and sealed, or the air crusts it.

The grill Hot subs, start to finish, plus bacon and steaks.

Grill basics and hot sub bread
  • Fast, calm, organized. Keep the tickets in the order they came in, and don't start a hot sub until you can give it your attention.
  • Scrape the grill as often as you can. A clean grill cooks quicker.

The bread is a pocket

  • Giants: run the knife across the front of the loaf, angled into the bread. Don't slice through the ends, and don't cut all the way through.

Temper it: warm, not toasted

  • Bottom side down on the grill, and turn it often so it warms evenly.

The slicer Cutting bread, slicing in order, and keeping the machine clean.

Slicing: speed starts before the rush

The video's order of things: safe, accurate, friendly, then fast. "Speed is a mentality."

  • Know the menu cold. When a guest says "13," you can't hesitate: every ingredient, in order. That's what the Drill tab is for.
  • Pre-cut a few loaves before the rush, regulars and giants, and restock in the lulls, so bread is grab and go.
  • Ask fewer questions. Don't ask which bread: slice it on white, and the guest will say if they want something else. Don't ask if they want peppers and onions on a hot sub: they'll tell you if they want it changed.
  • More than one at a time. Once the basics are in your hands, take more than one order and slice more than one sub at once.

Cutting bread for cold subs

A good cut gives a sub balanced between its top and bottom, and keeps your fingers out of the way.

  1. Free hand flat on top of the loaf, thumb and pinky lifted. Rest your palm with just enough pressure to hold the bread still. Giants and minis are cut this way as they are.
  2. Start at the butt of the bread: the round end, at the end closest to the guest.
  3. Saw, with the knife level, so the bread comes out in equal halves. Dragging the knuckles of your knife hand along the counter keeps the cut even.
  4. Saw all the way through the far end.
  5. Pass the lid to the sprinkler, set down as close to the sprinkling board as you can, and say which sub is coming and who it's for.

Regulars: cut a giant into two equal halves, point the round ends toward the guest, then cut each one the same way.

Stance, push, catch, flip
  • Athletic stance: feet about shoulder width apart and parallel, a little bend in the knees. Staggered feet make you lean into the slicer, your back pays for it, and someone walking behind you can trip on your foot.
  • A small step back from the slicer. Too close and you rock back and forth and bump into it.
  • Elbow down. With your elbow up, your shoulder and head drop and you lose the guest. Elbow down: shoulders back, head up, standing straight.
  • Let the slicer do the work. The blade is sharp. Push straight through; don't press down into it.

Push only the length of the product. Going end to end on every stroke is wasted motion.

  • The hands work together. Every time the right hand pushes forward, the left comes in under the blade to catch. As the left flips the slice onto the sub, the right is already coming back.
  • The right hand never stops. Stopping it while you flip is what breaks the rhythm.
  • Eyes: on the sub while you learn, since the meat comes out in the same spot every time. Then up, on the guest.

Meat case, guard, hands
  • Few steps to the meat case and back, and close the meat case door every time you take something out.
  • Place product in gently. Dropping or tossing it in wears the slicer out.
  • "Bring the guard to the party." The guard goes on top of the product every time. It holds the product in place and keeps you off the blade.
  • As the guard comes down, the left hand sets the dial. Setting it turns the slicer on.
  • Right thumb locked in behind the pusher deck, fingers resting on the guard. Not on the handle: the handle pulls your elbow up. A little pressure from your fingers gives a slightly thicker slice when you need one.
  • Taking product out: guard off, right hand slides the product into your left, back to the meat case, door closed. Never reach in with your left hand.

Setting the thickness, every day

Thickness changes the flavor and the value. Too thick: the flavor is off, the meat is hard to flower, and it costs the store. Too thin: there isn't enough meat on the sub, and the guest may not come back.

  • The scale first: power on, a sheet of paper down, then tare so the paper doesn't count.
  • Cheese (provolone and Swiss) is set with provolone: 5 slices should weigh 2 oz.
  • Meat is set with boiled ham: 8 slices should weigh 4.75 oz.
  • Heavy or light? Move the dial and slice again until it lands.
  • Every morning. The test slices get used: provolone goes to the grill for hot subs, ham goes on call-in orders or employee meals.

Slicing the sub

The transcript of the video spells it "flour." The move is flowering: the slice gathers on your fingertips instead of lying flat.

  • The slice guide is a guide, not a rule. It lists the slices for each sub. If a slice comes out as half a slice, add another.
  • Equal distribution. Every meat covers the sub end to end: the end nearest the guest and the end nearest you. Every bite has every product.
  • Proper sequence. Slice in the order the slice guide lists them.
  • One-meat subs go on in layers. The #7 gets about 10 slices of turkey, in 2 layers of about 5.
  • No double pumping: pushing through twice and catching once. It takes height off the sub, and height is the value the guest sees.

Weighing subs

Two reasons: the guest gets what they paid for, and the store doesn't give its food cost away.

  • Tell the guest what you're doing. Say you're putting their sub on the scale to check yourself and make sure they get enough meat and cheese. Without that, it looks like skimping.
  • Never take slices off. If anything, add one or two.
  • Weigh as many online, call-in and catering orders as you can.
  • Pick one sub a day (say the #8) and weigh as many of those as you can.
  • At least 3 subs every shift you slice.
  • Memorize the weights, so you aren't looking at the slice guide every time.

Sub in a tub
  1. Deli sheet flat on the counter. A tub always gets the regular-size portion.
  2. Cheese first, laid flat on the sheet.
  3. The flowered meats, flat on the cheese. On a #13: boiled ham, then prosciuttini.
  4. The round meats, diagonally on top. On a #13: cappacuolo, then salami, then pepperoni.
  5. Roll it up, neat and tight.
  6. Cut 5 times: 6 equal pieces.
  7. 2 pinches of lettuce in the tub (2 soufflé cups), and the pieces laid neatly on top.
  8. The sprinkler does it Mike's Way, the same as any regular. Lid closed tight. Fork and knife.

Hot subs: ask if they'd like lettuce on the bottom, then the hot sub mixture goes in the container.

Wiping down the slicer

When: after each order. Before a veggie sub. After any sub with roast beef. In any break in the rush. And always before you walk away.

  1. Turn the dial clockwise until the blade is completely closed. That turns the slicer off.
  2. Loosen the black knob on the side and let the pusher deck swing open. That locks the dial and the blade closed.
  3. The towel: soaked in warm sanitizer solution, wrung out well, folded into 12.

Then wipe, in this order:

Swing the pusher deck back into place and tighten the black knob.

Cleaning the slicer, every 4 hours

Like every food contact surface, the slicer gets fully taken apart, washed, rinsed and sanitized every 4 hours.

Have ready: 2 metal mesh safety gloves, a container of warm soapy water, one of rinse water, one of warm sanitizer solution, and clean towels, with one kept back to dry the slicer. Wash your hands and put on clean gloves first.

  1. Dial clockwise, blade closed, slicer off. Then unplug it.
  2. Black knob loose, pusher deck swung open.
  3. Small black knob on the guard: take off the spike plate. Then take off the blade plate. Carry both to the three-compartment sink to be washed, rinsed and sanitized.
  4. Metal mesh gloves on.
  5. The blade: a soapy towel folded in 12, from the center outward, slowly. The back of the blade the same way.
  6. Thread the towel from the front between the blade and the guard ring, and turn the blade and towel by hand, pressing the towel lightly against the inside of the ring.
  7. The rest, top down: pusher deck from the outside in, adjustable deck front to back, the side, the towel fed through underneath from front to back, then the bay.
  8. Rinse: all the same steps with a towel soaked in rinse water. Sanitize: all the same steps with a towel soaked in warm sanitizer.
  9. Dry it with the clean towel, put it back together, plug it in. Then clean the mesh gloves by their own instructions.
