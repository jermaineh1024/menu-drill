Your food cost goal

CrunchTime tells you what food cost should have been. Add 2 points. That's your goal. Then see how close the real number came.

  • Actual food cost is what it was for the period.
  • Theoretical is what it should have been.
  • Goal = theoretical + 2 points. The 2 points allow for necessary waste and other acceptable things, and make sure guests get enough meat and cheese.
  • The video's example: theoretical 25.71%, so the goal is 27.71%. Actual came in at 28.08%. That's 0.37 over. Food cost is high.
  • The goal belongs to that report only. Next period has a new theoretical and a new goal.
  • Low is as dangerous as high. A number under goal can mean guests are being shorted.

Red and black: reading variances

A variance is the gap between what was used and what should have been used. Red means too much went out. Black means too little did.

  • Red, a negative variance: too much of the product is being used. Example: turkey 78 lb in the red for a week. A turkey weighs about 10.5 lb. That's a whole turkey a day over-portioned or wasted.
  • Black, a positive variance: not enough is being used. Example: provolone 7 lb in the black. Subs went out with too little cheese.
  • Black is just as dangerous as red. It usually means guests aren't getting what they paid for.
  • Some loss is normal. You can't use every last bit of a turkey. 78 lb is far past normal.

First, is the report right?

  1. Is it explainable? A roast beef got burned and thrown out. Product was loaned to another store and never entered. These affect one period only.
  2. Did someone type a number wrong? 1 provolone where it should be 10.
  3. Are all the invoices in and reconciled? Dates, amounts, adjustments. Check the purchase journal.

If it's none of those, the report is accurate, and the answer is in operations: how the store is being run.

Where to look

Two thirds of food cost is meat, cheese and bread. Start there. Work on 3 to 5 variances a week, then check them again the next week.

The questions to ask on the floor

  • Are subs sliced correctly? Check slice thickness and the weight of the whole sub.
  • Are ends being sliced down? Meat and cheese ends pile up fast and get expensive.
  • Are recipes followed? Overcooked roast beef or bacon costs you. So does tuna made wrong.
  • Is everything rung up right? A #2 rung in for a #5, or a #7 for an #8, throws off the numbers. Discounts get rung in too. A free dozen cookies for a loyal catering customer is fine. Ring it up.
  • Is the Production Pro chart used every day? Too much bread or bacon made is waste.
  • Bread and meat both negative, week after week? That can mean improper cash handling or theft.
  • Is the team eating the stock? A cookie, a bag of chips, a soda, a strip of bacon. It adds up. They do what they see you do.
  • Are deliveries checked in properly? Damaged or missing product that isn't recorded drives cost up.

The training's line for this: pennies add up to dollars.

The food cost fitness form

When food cost is off and you don't know why, this form lists every cause it could be, each with a page of fixes.

  • The causes it covers: over-portioning and inventory controls. Excessive waste. Poor purchasing and receiving. Product theft. Improper cash handling. Incorrect pricing. Product mix.
  • Not sure which one? Assume you may have all of them.
  • Start with the one you know or suspect. Turn to its solution page and follow it.
  • Do what it takes to prevent every one of them, even the ones that look basic.
  • Still stuck? Ask another store manager, the franchisee or the area director.

Food cost is the biggest expense of running the store. Keeping it on goal is one of a manager's core jobs.
