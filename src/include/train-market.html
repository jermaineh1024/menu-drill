Four walls marketing

Everything a guest sees, touches and hears inside the store is marketing. A commercial gets 30 seconds. You get at least 3 minutes.

  • An impression is the feeling someone forms without even thinking about it. You get one chance at a first one.
  • The front door: is the glass clean? Is the handle clean?
  • The slicer: guests don't touch it, but they watch it. Keep it clean through the whole rush.
  • Tables: not sticky, and checked often.
  • Napkin dispensers: never dirty.
  • Bathrooms: the cleaner they are, the cleaner guests think the whole store is.
  • Displays: the cookie display, soda case and chip racks full and looking great at all times. That's what prompts the extra purchase.
  • You set the tone: a warm smile, a real greeting, some energy.

What should the logo mean to a guest? In the training's words, the experience and the people first, and then the sandwich.

The tools: points, texts, email, coupons, giving

Five things the store already has for bringing guests back. The strongest one is free: a smile.

  • Shore Points. Ask every guest. The simple way to explain it: buy 12 subs, get the 13th free. Twelve minis earn a mini, twelve regulars a regular, twelve giants a giant. Make sure members get their points every visit.
  • Text messages. A guest joins by text, and that signs them up for Shore Points too. Double points days are announced by text. The company sends one a month. The store manager or franchisee can send up to 3 more.
  • The email club. A sign-up sheet in the store. Monthly offers, and a free sub on the guest's birthday. When a birthday sub comes in, make a big deal of it.
  • Bounce back coupons. A stack of free chip and drink cards and $1 off cards at the register, handed out as a surprise. They bring people back.
  • Giving. "We give to give." March is the Month of Giving. On the Day of Giving, 100% of sales goes to local charities. Fundraising ties the store to its community.

Offers and sign-up details change. The video says about half of all transactions already belong to a member. Check what your store is running now.

A marketing program in 5 steps

Two goals: bring in people who've never tried you, and bring current guests in more often. Five steps, and then you do them again.

  1. Plan. Who goes out, and where. It should be someone who can be themselves away from the counter. A marketing map hangs in the back room. Know everything within 1 mile: every street, business and school.
  2. Prepare. Menus, flyers, free sub cards. Ordered and in stock before you go.
  3. Implement. Run a program from the local store marketing manual. Some happen inside the store, some outside it.
  4. Evaluate. After each visit, write notes on their business card: which free sub cards you left (say, 11 through 17), how many employees, do they hold meetings, do they cater. Log it on the tracking sheet. Did the cards come back? If not, why?
  5. Re-implement. Marketing is never one time. In retail, staff turn over about every 3 months, so the people you met are probably gone.
  • Ways to split the map: pie slices, puzzle pieces, or by kind of business (churches and schools, small businesses, large retailers).
  • Cards that never come back tell you something. Maybe that business can't get out for lunch, and online ordering or delivery would fit them.

Put the steps in order

Tap the steps in order.

Grassroots

Grassroots is walking into the businesses near your store, saying who you are, and inviting people in for lunch. You aren't selling anything.

  • New guests: buy them lunch. A free sub card gets the first try.
  • Current guests: give them options. If they can't get out, show them online ordering or delivery.
  • Catering is not grassroots. It follows by itself once people have tried the food.

Ground rules

  • Set a reasonable goal. Two hours out might be a couple hundred cards, each with a real conversation. Not a thousand cards dropped.
  • Relax and be yourself. You're meeting neighbors.
  • Be patient. They have customers and phones. You come second.
  • Smile, make eye contact.
  • Be able to say exactly where your store is.
  • Nervous is okay. It gets easier every time.

Planning it

  • Go when the store can spare you. After the opening is done and the second person is in. About 9 to 10:30 or 11.
  • Not on a truck day.
  • Trust the team. If you can't leave and stop checking your phone, it's not the day.
  • Bring: menus, free sub cards, and a way to show online ordering or delivery.
  • Keep it simple. A few offers, not fifteen. Fewer are easier to track.
  • Carry free sub cards every time you leave the store. The bank, the gas station, the gym. Invite someone in every day.

The visit: big stores, small shops, offices

Each kind of business has a best time to visit and one person worth knowing.

Big-box stores
When: early, right after they open. Who: the manager. You'll only reach one or two people, but the manager can bring you into a morning meeting or pass cards to the staff. Get a name, a number and a business card.
Small shops
When: any time of day. Who: the owner or manager, and then every employee. With a small staff you can invite each one in person.
Offices
When: it depends. A doctor's office first thing, before patients. A big office building any time. Who: the receptionist, who usually orders lunch. Be patient and kind. Dress the part: for office buildings, a pressed Oxford shirt.

What to say

  1. Your name, and that you're from the store down the street.
  2. "Have you ever been in?"
  3. If no: ask how many work there and if they can get out for lunch. Hand them a free sub card. No catches. "I'd love to make you lunch."
  4. If yes: thank them. Give them a card for their next visit. Ask to meet the rest of the staff.
  5. Ask for their business card before you go.

Catering comes up only when it fits. In the training's role play the manager already loved the food and mentioned monthly staff meetings, so offering a tray made sense.
