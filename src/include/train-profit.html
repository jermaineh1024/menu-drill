Run it like you own it

Profit is what happens when everything is done right. Act like the owner before you are one, and people notice.

  • Want a raise? Work as though you already have it.
  • Want to be the manager? Show a manager's habits now.
  • Want your own store someday? Take pride in every sub and every guest.

What that looks like

  • Look people in the eyes.
  • Speak with confidence, and with humility.
  • Make every sub with enough meat and enough juice, with urgency.
  • Seek out people who have done it before, and listen.
  • Make the people around you happy. Be the one everyone wants to work with, not the dark cloud.
  • Be responsible with your own money.
  • Be on the front line with your team. Lead by doing.
  • Be patient and humble. Doors open.

The four keys

Four numbers decide profit: sales, food cost, labor cost, and what's left. Take care of the first three and the fourth follows.

1. Top line sales
The store's total sales. Grow them by valuing every guest: the right amount of meat and cheese, enough juice, and a reason to come back. Four walls marketing and grassroots feed this.
2. Food cost
Order well, count weekly, read what CrunchTime tells you, and use the food cost fitness form when it's off.
3. Labor cost
Know the flow of your business, so people are there when guests are. Invest in training: a trained team lowers labor and usually helps sales and food cost too. Change the plan mid-shift when the day calls for it.
4. The bottom line
What's left. Protect it by watching the other three.

Bread math

Bread is on every sub, so it measures the whole business. Two numbers: sales per loaf, and loaves per day.

  • Average sales per loaf = the week's sales divided by the week's loaves. $16,500 on 1,000 loaves is $16.50. Size and add-ons are already inside that number.
  • Average bread per day = the week's loaves divided by 7. 1,000 loaves is 143 a day.
  • The goal: 10 more loaves a day. At $16.50 that's $165 a day, or $1,155 a week. The week goes from $16,500 to $17,655.

Raising sales per loaf

  • Offer the add-ons: chips and a drink, a fresh baked cookie. Ask without sounding like a recording.
  • Ring up the extras: extra meat, extra cheese, bacon.
  • Minis and regulars: three minis bring in more than one giant. So do two regulars.
  • Pricing: the home office sets recommended prices.

The P&L, top half

The P&L is a monthly report card for the store's money. Sales at the top, what's left at the bottom, and every cost in between.

Sales

  • Gross sales: the value of everything the team made, before discounts and coupons.
  • Net sales: the money that actually came in.
  • The gap is your discounts and coupons. Watch it month to month, above all after a promotion. Example: $94,587 gross, $89,976 net.

Cost of goods

  • Food is the biggest number, and one you control every day. It should sit about 2 points above CrunchTime's theoretical. Example: 26.1%.
  • Paper usually runs 1.5 to 2%. Nearer 2.5% usually means a lot of catering: boxes, bags, supplies. Example: 1.9%.

Facility: mostly fixed

  • Rent: the aim is 8% of sales or less. It includes CAM (common area maintenance: landscaping, snow removal, the roof), taxes and insurance. Example: 4.2%.
  • Repairs and maintenance: kept in line by following the preventative maintenance schedule.
  • Computer: fees for the register software, CrunchTime, the online ordering app.
  • Utilities: usually 1.5 to 2.5%. Watch for jumps. A water bill that shoots up may be a leaky toilet.
  • Waste removal and insurance.
  • You can't change these day to day, but habits help: equipment kept up, the bread oven off when it isn't in use. Example total: 7%.

The P&L, bottom half

Labor is the second biggest cost and the other one you control. Below it sit supplies and fees. What's left is called EBITDA.

Labor

  • Hourly labor: usually 12 to 16% of sales. This one line is what people mean by "the labor number."
  • Hourly plus management: usually 20 to 22%. The two are kept on separate lines so problems are easy to see.
  • Payroll taxes rise and fall with payroll dollars.
  • Workers' compensation comes down when training and safety prevent injuries.
  • Example, everything in: 22.5%.

Operations

  • Over and short: the cash that should be in the drawer against what was deposited. With good cash handling it barely moves. The example is $1.81 short for the month.
  • Supplies: linens (towels, aprons, floor mats), office supplies, gloves (about a quarter to half a percent of sales), cleaning supplies, small kitchen tools.
  • The rest: delivery and catering service fees, local advertising, accounting, training, licenses, uniforms, donations.
  • Theft has its own line: cash that went missing between the drawer and the deposit.

Fees to the brand

The royalty. Corporate advertising, which is what you see inside the store. The national media fee: TV, radio, billboards.

EBITDA

  • It stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. In plain words: what the store earned from running, before loans and taxes.
  • The store's P&L stops there on purpose. It shows only what the people in the store can affect. Example: 25.8%.
  • A healthy P&L: grow sales, keep food within 2 points of theoretical, forecast labor and adjust during the week. Teach your team the parts they affect.

The weekly review

One sheet, once a week: find about a dozen numbers, write them side by side with the weeks before, and circle the ones to fix.

From the register's reports, for last Monday through Sunday

  • Sales, ticket count, average check.
  • Bread, average bread per day (bread divided by 7), average sales per loaf.
  • Discounts and coupons.
  • Voids, refunds, cancelled tickets.
  • Shore Points members.

From CrunchTime

  • Actual food cost, theoretical food cost, and the variance between them. Inventory, Reports, Actual Theoretical Cost.
  • Actual labor percent. Labor, Reports, Daily Payroll Control.

Then

Line the week up next to the weeks before it. The sheet holds up to five. Circle any number that needs work, and improve it during the week you're in.
