Forecast the week's sales

Every schedule starts with one number: what you expect to sell. Labor is the store's second highest cost, and it should never be a surprise.

Two ways to get the number

  • The 4-week average. Add the last 4 weeks of sales. Divide by 4. In the video that comes to $17,535.
  • Last year plus growth. Take the same week last year and add the percent sales are up. Your franchisee has that percent. $16,659.07 plus 5% ($832.95) is $17,492.02.
  • Both land close together. That's a good sign the forecast is reasonable.

Then adjust for what you know

  • Time of year: seasons, holidays, students arriving or leaving.
  • Weather: look at the 10-day forecast. It will change, but it beats going in blind.
  • Community events: a high school football game, a fair, a charity function.
  • Large catering orders that are out of the ordinary.

CrunchTime can forecast for you. The training wants you to know the math behind it anyway. It calls this "knowing the long division."

The coverage chart

For each hour of each day, decide how many fully trained people the store needs. Draw one line for each person's shift.

  1. Get each day's sales. Average the last 4 Mondays for Monday, and so on. The example Monday is $2,249.37, rounded to $2,250.
  2. Draw the opener's line. In the example the manager works 8 to 4. Write the hours: 8.
  3. Add the second person. A shift leader, 9:15 to 1:30. That's 4.25 hours.
  4. Add people as business picks up. A team member 10:30 to 2:30. Another 11:30 to 2. The assistant manager 11:45 to 7:30.
  5. Check the lunch peak. 11:45 to 1:30 has 5 people on.
  6. Draw dinner and close. A shift leader 4 to 9:15. One more person 6 to 9:15. That gives 3 for the dinner peak and 2 to finish closing.
  7. Check the quiet hours too. Enough people to serve guests and finish checklists?

Two rules that never bend

  • At least 2 people in the store during business hours and through closing. The store never closes with fewer than 2.
  • A manager, assistant manager or shift leader is in the store at all times.

The hours and times are the video's example. Every store's busy hours are different, and that's what the chart is for.

Labor percent

Add up what the day's shifts cost. Divide by the day's forecasted sales. That's the labor percent, and you know it before the day happens.

  1. For each person: hours times wage = their pay for the day.
  2. Add everyone's pay. The example Monday comes to $335.75.
  3. Divide by the day's forecasted sales, $2,250. You get 0.1492.
  4. Move the decimal two places right: 14.92%.
  • For the week: add all seven days of pay, then divide by the week's forecasted sales.
  • The goal changes day to day, because sales and coverage do.
  • Looks high or low? Go back to the lines you drew.
  • Try the quarter hour. Start at 10:15 in place of 10:00. End at 2:15 in place of 2:30. Fifteen minutes here and there is a big saving over time.
  • Fully trained people lower labor cost.
  • You don't redraw the chart every week. Do it until you know your store's coverage, and again before any change.

Schedule habits, and the look back

Write it the same time each week, check it before you post it, and after the week ends, ask what it taught you.

Writing it

  • Same time every week. The checklist suggests Thursday afternoon, which leaves time to review and post.
  • Put the shifts into CrunchTime. It shows the forecast and the daily and weekly labor percent.
  • Double-check before posting.
  • Managers work the peaks. That's where a manager can grow sales.
  • Stagger the manager, assistant manager and shift leaders, so strong leadership is always in the store.
  • Enough people for safety.
  • Breaks that follow your local labor laws. That protects everyone.

Ask for each day

  • When do the opener and the second person arrive?
  • When does it pick up enough to need a third?
  • When do the lunch and dinner rushes begin and end?
  • How many are needed in the afternoon to serve guests and finish checklists?
  • Do you need more than 3 for the dinner rush?
  • When do the closers usually finish?

After the week

  • Was the sales forecast accurate?
  • Were there a lot of changes after it was posted?
  • Did weather change it?
  • Did something happen that could have been planned for?
  • What did you learn that you can use next week?

Scheduling is as much about people as it is about money: an honest look at your team's talent, balanced against the store's goals, so the guest always gets great service.
