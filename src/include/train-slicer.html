Know your slicer

The slicer on the line is a Bizerba GSP H, a gravity slicer with a 13-inch blade. The maker's name is on the front of the base. Here are its parts, by the names the training uses.

  1. Pusher deck. The ribbed tray the product lies on. You push it to slice.
  2. Green handle, on the pusher deck.
  3. Guard. It comes down on top of the product, holds it in place and keeps your hand off the blade.
  4. Red handle, on the guard.
  5. Spike plate, under the guard. Its spikes grip the product. The small black knob on the guard holds it on, and it comes off for the 4-hour clean.
  6. Black knob on the side. Loosen it and the pusher deck swings open for wiping and cleaning.
  7. Dial. Sets the thickness. Clockwise all the way closes the blade. The numbers are millimeters, up to 24.
  8. Buttons. Green I switches the blade on, and the white light stays on while it runs. Red O switches it off.
  9. Blade end. The blade is back here, with the guard ring around its edge and the blade plate over its center.
  10. The sticker: sharpen and lube daily. See Sharpen and oil the slicer.

How it behaves

  • Closing the dial switches the blade off. Turn it clockwise until the blade is completely closed.
  • The pusher deck only swings open with the dial closed all the way. Once it's open, the dial and the blade stay locked closed.
  • Don't drive it from the red or green handle. It pulls your elbow up. Thumb locked in behind the pusher deck, index finger pointing forward, the other fingers on top of the guard.
  • Go by the scale and the tomato slice, never the number on the dial. The thickness gets checked every morning.

For whoever is in charge

  • Sharpening grinds the blade down over time. When the gap between its edge and the guard ring reaches 6 millimeters (about 1/4 inch), the sharpener can't sharpen it any more and the blade has to be replaced.
  • Only Bizerba's service changes a blade. That's Bizerba's safety rule, from its manual.

Slicing: speed starts before the rush

The video's order of things: safe, accurate, friendly, then fast. "Speed is a mentality."

  • Know the menu cold. When a guest says "13," you can't hesitate: every ingredient, in order. That's what the Drill tab is for.
  • Pre-cut a few loaves before the rush, regulars and giants, and restock in the lulls, so bread is grab and go.
  • Ask fewer questions. Don't ask which bread: slice it on white, and the guest will say if they want something else. Don't ask if they want peppers and onions on a hot sub: they'll tell you if they want it changed.
  • More than one at a time. Once the basics are in your hands, take more than one order and slice more than one sub at once.

Cutting bread for cold subs

A good cut gives a sub balanced between its top and bottom, and keeps your fingers out of the way.

  1. Free hand flat on top of the loaf, thumb and pinky lifted. Rest your palm with just enough pressure to hold the bread still. Giants and minis are cut this way as they are.
  2. Start at the butt of the bread: the round end, at the end closest to the guest.
  3. Saw, with the knife level, so the bread comes out in equal halves. Dragging the knuckles of your knife hand along the counter keeps the cut even.
  4. Saw all the way through the far end.
  5. Pass the lid to the sprinkler, set down as close to the sprinkling board as you can, and say which sub is coming and who it's for.

Regulars: cut a giant into two equal halves, point the round ends toward the guest, then cut each one the same way.

Stance, push, catch, flip
  • Athletic stance: feet about shoulder width apart and parallel, a little bend in the knees. Staggered feet make you lean into the slicer, your back pays for it, and someone walking behind you can trip on your foot.
  • A small step back from the slicer. Too close and you rock back and forth and bump into it.
  • Elbow down. With your elbow up, your shoulder and head drop and you lose the guest. Elbow down: shoulders back, head up, standing straight.
  • Let the slicer do the work. The blade is sharp. Push straight through; don't press down into it.

Push only the length of the product. Going end to end on every stroke is wasted motion.

  • The hands work together. Every time the right hand pushes forward, the left comes in under the blade to catch. As the left flips the slice onto the sub, the right is already coming back.
  • The right hand never stops. Stopping it while you flip is what breaks the rhythm.
  • Eyes: on the sub while you learn, since the meat comes out in the same spot every time. Then up, on the guest.

Meat case, guard, hands
  • Few steps to the meat case and back, and close the meat case door every time you take something out.
  • Place product in gently. Dropping or tossing it in wears the slicer out.
  • "Bring the guard to the party." The guard goes on top of the product every time. It holds the product in place and keeps you off the blade.
  • As the guard comes down, the left hand sets the dial. Setting it turns the slicer on.
  • Right thumb locked in behind the pusher deck, index finger pointing forward, the other fingers resting on top of the guard. Don't drive the slicer from the red or green handle: it pulls your elbow up. A little pressure from your fingers gives a slightly thicker slice when you need one.
  • Taking product out: guard off, right hand slides the product into your left, back to the meat case, door closed. Never reach in with your left hand.

Setting the thickness, every day

Thickness changes the flavor and the value. Too thick: the flavor is off, the meat is hard to flower, and it costs the store. Too thin: there isn't enough meat on the sub, and the guest may not come back.

  • The scale first: power on, a sheet of paper down, then tare so the paper doesn't count.
  • Cheese (provolone and Swiss) is set with provolone: 5 slices should weigh 2 oz.
  • Meat is set with boiled ham: 8 slices should weigh 4.75 oz.
  • The slicing sheet gives a shorter check too: 3 slices of ham should weigh 1.8 oz.
  • Heavy or light? Move the dial and slice again until it lands.
  • Every morning. The test slices get used: provolone goes to the grill for hot subs, ham goes on call-in orders or employee meals.

Slicing the sub

The transcript of the video spells it "flour." The move is flowering: the slice gathers on your fingertips instead of lying flat.

  • The slice guide is a guide, not a rule. It lists the slices for each sub. If a slice comes out as half a slice, add another.
  • Equal distribution. Every meat covers the sub end to end: the end nearest the guest and the end nearest you. Every bite has every product.
  • Start at the end closest to the guest, cover your ends, and build up, not out.
  • Proper sequence. Slice in the order the slice guide lists them.
  • One-meat subs go on in layers. The #7 gets about 10 slices of turkey, in 2 layers of about 5.
  • No double pumping: pushing through twice and catching once. It takes height off the sub, and height is the value the guest sees.

Weighing subs

Two reasons: the guest gets what they paid for, and the store doesn't give its food cost away.

  • Tell the guest what you're doing. Say you're putting their sub on the scale to check yourself and make sure they get enough meat and cheese. Without that, it looks like skimping.
  • Never take slices off. If anything, add one or two.
  • Weigh as many online, call-in and catering orders as you can.
  • Pick one sub a day (say the #8) and weigh as many of those as you can.
  • At least 3 subs every shift you slice.
  • Memorize the weights, so you aren't looking at the slice guide every time.

Slice weights, sub by sub

What the cheese and meat on each cold sub should weigh, for a giant, a regular and a mini. These are the numbers on the slicing sheet on the meat case door.

  • Learn the top sellers first. The sign by the slicer lists six: the #7, #13, #8, #9, #6 and #3.
  • Provolone is the same on almost every sub: 2 oz on a giant, 1.2 oz on a regular, 0.8 oz on a mini. The #14 Veggie gets more: 4 oz, 2 oz and 1.2 oz.
  • Cappacuolo never changes: 1.4 oz, 0.85 oz, 0.55 oz, on every sub that has it.
  • A giant is not double a regular. The #7 is 9.2 oz as a giant and 5.5 oz as a regular. Learn each size.
  • Bacon is counted, not weighed. A BLT gets 12 slices, 6 or 4. A club gets 4, 2 or 1.
  • Tuna goes on in an even layer: 10 oz, 5.5 oz, 3.25 oz.
  • Peppers on the #14 are counted in rings: 5, 3 or 2.

Setting up the meat case

Five rules from the poster on the meat case door.

  • On delivery, weigh it and date it. Write the weight and the delivery date on each product. The use-by date is already printed on it.
  • Empty and clean the meat case every week.
  • Rotate new product into the display.
  • Extra product goes to the walk-in.
  • Leave a 3-inch gap between the display and the front glass, so air can flow. Don't line the case with paper.

The poster also shows which meat sits where, for a 6-foot case and a 4-foot case. Follow the one on your door.

Sub in a tub
  1. Deli sheet flat on the counter. A tub always gets the regular-size portion.
  2. Cheese first, laid flat on the sheet.
  3. The flowered meats, flat on the cheese. On a #13: boiled ham, then prosciuttini.
  4. The round meats, diagonally on top. On a #13: cappacuolo, then salami, then pepperoni.
  5. Roll it up, neat and tight.
  6. Cut 5 times: 6 equal pieces.
  7. 2 pinches of lettuce in the tub (2 soufflé cups), and the pieces laid neatly on top.
  8. The sprinkler does it Mike's Way, the same as any regular. Lid closed tight. Fork and knife.

Hot subs: ask if they'd like lettuce on the bottom, then the hot sub mixture goes in the container.

Wiping down the slicer

When: after each order. Before a veggie sub. After any sub with roast beef. In any break in the rush. And always before you walk away.

  1. Turn the dial clockwise until the blade is completely closed. That turns the slicer off.
  2. Loosen the black knob on the side and let the pusher deck swing open. That locks the dial and the blade closed.
  3. The towel: soaked in warm sanitizer solution, wrung out well, folded into 12.

Then wipe, in this order:

Swing the pusher deck back into place and tighten the black knob.

Cleaning the slicer, every 4 hours

Like every food contact surface, the slicer gets fully taken apart, washed, rinsed and sanitized every 4 hours.

The four full cleans on the sign by the slicer: after prepping and sharpening (10 to 11 a.m.), after the lunch rush (2 to 3 p.m.), before the dinner rush (5 to 6 p.m.), and at closing. Write each time on the slicer cleaning tracking form.

Have ready: 2 metal mesh safety gloves, a container of warm soapy water, one of rinse water, one of warm sanitizer solution, and clean towels, with one kept back to dry the slicer. Wash your hands and put on clean gloves first.

  1. Dial clockwise, blade closed, slicer off. Then unplug it.
  2. Black knob loose, pusher deck swung open.
  3. Small black knob on the guard: take off the spike plate. Then take off the blade plate. Carry both to the three-compartment sink to be washed, rinsed and sanitized.
  4. Metal mesh gloves on.
  5. The blade: a soapy towel folded in 12, from the center outward, slowly. The back of the blade the same way.
  6. Thread the towel from the front between the blade and the guard ring, and turn the blade and towel by hand, pressing the towel lightly against the inside of the ring.
  7. The rest, top down: pusher deck from the outside in, adjustable deck front to back, the side, the towel fed through underneath from front to back, then the bay.
  8. Rinse: all the same steps with a towel soaked in rinse water. Sanitize: all the same steps with a towel soaked in warm sanitizer.
  9. Dry it with the clean towel, put it back together, plug it in. Then clean the mesh gloves by their own instructions.
